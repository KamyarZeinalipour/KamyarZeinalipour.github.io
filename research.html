Research

Five lines of work. They share one question: can a model produce something a person would actually use without fixing it first, and how would we know? Each line has a figure you can play with, built from the released data where there is some.

Open models for Europe's languages

With the team at Villanova.AI I worked on Multi-SFT, permissively licensed multilingual instruction-following data. All four Villanova2 models cite it: open-weight models trained in Italy for English, Italian, German, Spanish and French, a 15.5B mixture of experts and a dense 2.4B, released under Apache 2.0.

Instruction data decides what a base model learns to do when asked. Making it permissively licensed is what lets the resulting models be truly open.

Benchmarks for languages that have none

Most evaluation suites stop at English. I built commonsense benchmarks for Farsi and Arabic, a dialogue benchmark across Egyptian, Maghrebi and Modern Standard Arabic, a dialogue benchmark for Farsi, and instruction data that gets Sindhi models to reason in steps.

The figure is the simplest ArabicSense task: two sentences, one of which cannot be true. A model that has read a lot of Arabic text should never pick the wrong one; the paper measures how often they still do.

Study material, generated and checked

A crossword clue is a small, strict test of a generator: it has to be true, it has to point at exactly one answer, and it has to be worth solving. I started there with Italian, then rebuilt the pipeline for Arabic and Turkish, where the puzzle conventions and the morphology are not the same.

The same question carried into quizzes, Persian multiple-choice items, pedagogical hints that help without giving the answer away, and syntax feedback on student essays.

Crosswords in Italian, Arabic and Turkish. Quizzes in Turkish. Questions in Persian. Hints in MultiHint-9K.

Measuring what models really do

Sixty people solved 160 Italian clues while we recorded how long each took. Difficulty tracks the surprisal a model assigns to the answer: how unexpected the answer is, given the clue. That gives a puzzle generator an objective dial instead of a setter's instinct.

Try the clues before you reveal the answers. Higher surprisal predicts that people will find a clue harder: in the paper, the correlation with accuracy reaches r = -0.62 for nominal clues.

Related work asks where models break: plural nouns with no singular, across many languages at once, and whether iterative in-context learning helps abstract reasoning on algebra.

Science and industry

The same methods, pointed at other data. To a language model a protein is a sentence in a twenty-letter alphabet, which is how I approached protein design and PharMistral, a model that proposes candidate ligands for a protein target from its sequence alone.

On the industrial side: knowledge graphs built from plant documentation, then written back out as text to measure what survives the round trip; question answering over Italian regulatory documents; and emission forecasting for gas turbines, deployed at Baker Hughes.

Code and models

Each paper has one repository and one model collection, and the two link to each other. Most released checkpoints are LoRA adapters, so the work can be reproduced on a single GPU rather than a cluster.
