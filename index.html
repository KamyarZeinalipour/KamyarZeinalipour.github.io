Four lines of work

They share one question: can a language model produce material a teacher would hand to a student without editing it first, and how would we know?

Study material, generated and checked

Crosswords in Italian, Arabic and Turkish. Quizzes in Turkish, multiple-choice questions in Persian.

A crossword clue is a small, strict test of a generator: it has to be true, it has to point at exactly one answer, and it has to be worth solving. I started there, with Italian, then rebuilt the pipeline for Arabic and Turkish, where the puzzle conventions and the morphology are not the same.

The same question carried into quizzes, Persian multiple-choice items, pedagogical hints that help without giving the answer away, and syntax feedback on student essays. Each one shipped with the data and the fine-tuned models, so the claim can be checked.

Benchmarks for languages that have none

Most evaluation suites stop at English. I built commonsense benchmarks for Farsi and Arabic, a dialogue benchmark covering Egyptian, Maghrebi and Modern Standard Arabic, and instruction data that gets Sindhi models to reason in steps.

Every dataset and adapter is public.

Measuring what models really do

Sixty people solved 160 Italian clues while we recorded how long each took. Clue difficulty tracks the surprisal a model assigns to the answer, which gives puzzle generators an objective dial instead of a setter's instinct.

Related work asks where models break: plural nouns that have no singular, across many languages at once.

Science and industry

The same fine-tuning methods, pointed at other data. Protein sequences designed with language models. Knowledge graphs built from industrial text and turned back into text to see what survived the round trip. Italian question answering over pharmaceutical regulatory documents.

About

I finished my Ph.D. at the University of Siena in February 2026, supervised by Marco Gori, and I stayed on at DIISM as a postdoctoral researcher with Marco Maggini. Before Siena I studied applied mathematics here and applied statistics at the University of Hormozgan, which is where the habit of checking a claim against data comes from.

I have spent stretches as a visiting researcher at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, at Inria in Nice and at the University of Gothenburg, and I work as a generative AI engineer at YUKAI! in Siena, where the research methods meet deadlines and real users. I have supervised ten master's students through their theses.

If you want to collaborate on educational NLP, on a language that needs resources, or on evaluation that goes beyond a leaderboard, write to me.
